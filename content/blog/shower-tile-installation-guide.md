## How Much Does Shower Tile Installation Cost?

A custom tile shower often costs **$4,000–$10,000** in Southwest Florida. It depends on a few key things:

-   **Shower size.** A bigger shower means more tile and more work. It also means more area to seal.
-   **Tile.** Ceramic and porcelain cost less than stone. They cost less than handmade or imported tile too.
-   **Extras.** Wall shelves, seats, and mosaic bands add work time. So does mixing tile on one wall.
-   **Entry style.** A curbless shower needs more exact slope work. It often needs a long, thin drain. That costs more than a shower with a curb.
-   **The job.** A tub-to-shower swap is not the same work as a full gut and rebuild. The price shows that. Is your shower part of a bigger bathroom job? Then see our [bathroom tile installation](/services/bathroom-tile-installation) page.

Some contractors give a firm number over the phone without seeing the space. They are guessing. A real quote comes after an in-home estimate. There, the contractor can measure and talk about tile with you. They can also plan for anything odd about your plumbing or framing.

> **Rule of thumb:** is a quote far lower than the rest? Then ask what's being skipped. The seal layer and the sloped base are the two corners most often cut. And they are the two things that keep a shower from leaking into your walls and floor.

## The Installation Process, Step by Step

A good tile shower job follows the same steps. That is true for a simple update or a full custom build:

1.  **Free in-home estimate.** A contractor measures the space. They talk about the layout. Curb or curbless? Where do the shelf and seat go? Then you get a firm, written quote. A number said out loud is not enough.
2.  **Tear-out and framing.** The old shower or tub walls come out. Framing changes are made for a seat, a shelf, or a new layout.
3.  **Seal layer and slope.** A seal layer goes over the base. The shower floor is sloped the right way to the drain. We explain this step below. It is the one that decides if your shower lasts.
4.  **Layout and setting.** The tile is laid out dry first. That way cuts match on each side. The tile doesn't start in an odd spot in a corner. Then it is set with the right thin-set and trowel for that tile.
5.  **Grout, seal, and walk-through.** Grout and sealer go on. Then a final walk-through checks that it all looks and works the way it should. Only then is the job done.

## Why Sealing Out Water Is the Step That Matters Most

A shower gets soaked with running water each day. So it is not like most other surfaces in your home. Tile itself is not waterproof. Grout lines and even the tile face let some water through over time. What keeps water out of your walls and floor is behind the tile. It is the seal layer and the sloped base. It is not the tile.

Ask each contractor bidding on your job about this. What seal layer do they use? How do they build the sloped base? A straight answer is a good sign. A fuzzy one isn't.

## Choosing the Right Shower Tile

Here are the most common shower tiles, from lowest to highest cost:

-   **Ceramic tile.** It costs the least. It comes in lots of colors and patterns. It is a solid choice for most showers.
-   **Porcelain tile.** It is denser than ceramic and keeps out more water. It comes in big sizes. It comes in wood-look and stone-look styles too. Those have gotten very popular for modern showers.
-   **Stone** (marble, travertine, slate). It is the top-end choice. Its look is hard to copy. But it most often needs sealing. It needs more care over time than ceramic or porcelain.
-   **Glass and mosaic tile.** They most often go in an accent band, a shelf, or a shower floor. They are not often used across a whole wall. Mosaic has more grout lines, so your feet grip better.

For the shower floor, grip matters more than anywhere else in the room. Small tile like mosaic, penny round, or hex has more grout lines. That most often gives better grip than big tile on a wet, sloped floor.

## Curbless vs. Classic Shower Entries

A classic shower has a curb. That is a raised lip, most often a few inches tall. It keeps water inside the shower pan. A curbless shower has no curb at all. So the shower floor is level with the bathroom floor.

Curbless showers have become popular. People like their clean, open look. They are easy to walk into too. But they need a more exact slope plan. They most often need a long, thin drain along one edge. A center drain is not enough, since there's no curb to hold water in. That extra care is part of why curbless showers most often cost more. The sealing and layout work is harder. It is not just a style choice.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

-   **Skipping a written list of the work.** A spoken deal leaves too much room to argue later about what was promised.
-   **Choosing on price alone.** The cheapest bid is often cheap because something got cut. Most often, it's the sealing.
-   **Forgetting about grip on the shower floor.** A pretty big tile that's slick when wet is a real safety problem. It's not just a matter of looks.
-   **Not planning shelves and seats ahead of time.** These must be framed and sealed the right way before tile goes up. They are much harder and cost more to add later.
-   **Hiring a contractor with no license or insurance.** If something goes wrong, you have no real way to get it fixed. See our [guide to choosing a tile contractor](/blog/how-to-choose-a-tile-contractor). It shows what to check before you sign anything.
