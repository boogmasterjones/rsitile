If you're planning a shower tile installation, the two questions on your mind are probably **what will it cost** and **how do I make sure it's done right**. This guide answers both. We've installed custom tile showers across Southwest Florida from our home base in [Port Charlotte](/locations/port-charlotte) for more than 30 years, and this is the same information we walk homeowners through during a free estimate — written down so you can go into the process informed, whether you hire us or someone else.

## How Much Does Shower Tile Installation Cost?

A custom shower tile installation typically costs **$4,000–$10,000** in Southwest Florida, depending on a few key factors:

-   **Shower size** — a larger footprint means more tile, more labor, and a bigger waterproofing area.
-   **Tile material** — ceramic and porcelain run less expensive than natural stone or handmade/imported tile.
-   **Features** — niches, built-in benches, mosaic accent bands, and multiple tile types on one wall all add labor time.
-   **Entry style** — a curbless or zero-entry shower requires more precise slope work and often a linear drain, which adds cost over a standard curbed entry.
-   **Scope** — a tub-to-shower conversion and a full gut-and-rebuild involve different work, and the price reflects that. If your shower is part of a larger bathroom project, see our [bathroom tile installation](/services/bathroom-tile-installation) page.

Any contractor who gives you a firm number over the phone without seeing the space is guessing. A real quote comes after an in-home estimate where the contractor can measure, discuss material options with you, and account for anything unusual about your existing plumbing or framing.

> **Rule of thumb:** if a quote is dramatically lower than everyone else's, ask what's being skipped. Waterproofing membrane and correct pre-slope are the two most common corners cut to hit a lowball price — and they're the two things that actually prevent a shower from leaking into your walls and subfloor.

## The Installation Process, Step by Step

A properly installed shower tile job follows the same general sequence, whether it's a simple refresh or a full custom build:

1.  **Free in-home estimate.** A contractor measures the space, discusses layout options (curb vs. curbless, niche placement, bench), and provides a firm, written quote — not a verbal ballpark.
2.  **Demolition and framing.** The old shower or tub surround comes out, along with any framing changes needed for a bench, niche, or altered layout.
3.  **Waterproofing and pre-slope.** A waterproofing membrane goes over the substrate, and the shower floor is sloped correctly toward the drain. This is the step described in detail below — it's the one that determines whether your shower lasts.
4.  **Layout and setting.** Tile is dry-fit and planned so cuts land symmetrically (not started awkwardly in a corner), then set with the correct thin-set and trowel size for the material.
5.  **Grouting, sealing, and walkthrough.** Grout and sealant are applied, and a final walkthrough confirms everything looks and functions the way it should before the job is called done.

## Why Waterproofing Is the Step That Matters Most

A shower is submerged in running water every single day, which puts it in a different category from almost any other surface in your home. Tile itself isn't waterproof — grout lines and even the tile's surface allow some moisture through over time. What actually keeps water out of your walls and subfloor is the waterproofing membrane and correct pre-slope _behind_ the tile, not the tile itself.

 Ask any contractor bidding your job exactly what waterproofing system they use and how the pre-slope is built — a straight answer is a good sign; a vague one isn't.

## Choosing the Right Shower Tile Material

The most common shower tile materials, roughly in order of cost:

-   **Ceramic tile** — the most budget-friendly option, widely available in a huge range of colors and patterns. A solid choice for most standard showers.
-   **Porcelain tile** — denser and more water-resistant than ceramic, including large-format and wood-look or stone-look options that have gotten very popular for modern showers.
-   **Natural stone** (marble, travertine, slate) — the premium option, with a look that's hard to replicate, but it typically needs sealing and more ongoing maintenance than ceramic or porcelain.
-   **Glass and mosaic tile** — usually used as an accent band, niche liner, or shower floor (mosaic's smaller grout lines improve slip resistance underfoot) rather than across an entire wall.

For the shower floor specifically, slip resistance matters more than anywhere else in the room — smaller-format tile (mosaic, penny round, hexagon) with more grout lines generally provides better traction than large-format tile on a wet, sloped surface.

## Curbless vs. Traditional Shower Entries

A traditional shower has a curb — a raised lip, usually a few inches tall — that contains water inside the shower pan. A curbless (or "zero-entry") shower removes that curb entirely, so the shower floor is flush with the bathroom floor.

Curbless showers have become popular for both their clean, open look and their walk-in accessibility, but they require more precise slope planning and typically a linear drain (a long, narrow drain along one edge) rather than a center drain, since there's no curb to help contain water. That added precision is part of why curbless entries usually cost more than a standard curbed shower — it's more demanding waterproofing and layout work, not just a design choice.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

-   **Skipping a written scope of work.** A verbal agreement leaves too much room for disagreement later about what was actually promised.
-   **Choosing based on price alone.** The cheapest bid is often cheap because something — usually waterproofing — got cut.
-   **Ignoring slip resistance on the shower floor.** A beautiful large-format tile that's slippery when wet is a real safety issue, not just an aesthetic tradeoff.
-   **Not planning niche and bench locations in advance.** These need to be framed and waterproofed correctly before tile goes up — they're much harder (and more expensive) to add after the fact.
-   **Hiring an unlicensed or uninsured contractor.** If something goes wrong, you have no real recourse. See our [guide to choosing a tile contractor](/blog/how-to-choose-a-tile-contractor) for what to check before you sign anything.
